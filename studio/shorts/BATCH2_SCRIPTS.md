# Batch 2: ten scripts (awaiting approval)

Same format as "A Whale of a Deal": ~55-65 s, ~3.7 words/s, our own characters and staging, the same narrator voice.
Beat formula: hook, setting, escalation, snap, "Luckily / Unluckily", one everyday analogy, consequence, callback.
Every joke is a researched fact. Lines marked ⚠ rest on a single source or on general knowledge, not a checked source.

## 1. The Building That Melted a Car
Facts: 20 Fenchurch Street, London ("Walkie-Talkie"), 37 storeys, concave south face. Sept 2013: Martin Lindsay's
Jaguar XJ, parked about an hour, had its wing mirror, badge and side panels melted. Nearby businesses reported blistered
paint, broken tiles, singed fabric. A TV crew fried an egg in the reflected beam ⚠ (one source). The City suspended three
parking bays; a sunshade (brise-soleil) was fitted. The building won the Carbuncle Cup (worst new building).
Sources: National Geographic (2013-09-04); Channel 4 News; ITV News; ArchDaily.

In 2013, a skyscraper in London melted a car, without touching it.
The new tower at 20 Fenchurch Street had a front that curved inward, like a giant spoon standing on its handle.
One sunny afternoon, Martin Lindsay parked his Jaguar across the street.
He came back an hour later. The wing mirror had melted. So had the badge. And the side panels.
The shops nearby had blistered paint, cracked tiles and scorched fabric.
Then a TV crew showed up and fried an egg on the pavement.
Luckily, nobody was standing in the beam.
Unluckily, people kept parking in it.
You see, a flat window bounces sunlight back in every direction it came from.
A curved one bounces it all to the same spot.
It's a magnifying glass, made of offices.
The city closed the parking spaces, and the building got a giant sunshade bolted to its face.
And later, it won an award: the Carbuncle Cup, for the worst new building in Britain.

## 2. The Hotel That Went Up
Facts: Chicago raised its street grade in the 1850s-60s to fit sewers. 1861: the six-storey brick Tremont House was
lifted about six feet by Ely, Smith and George Pullman, with guests staying in their rooms; Pullman had promised not to
disturb a guest or break a pane of glass. Thousands of jackscrews (5,000 in several sources), turned a half turn at a
time on a signal by hundreds of men (crew size varies 500-1,200). Pullman later built the Pullman sleeping car.
Sources: Wikipedia "Tremont House (Chicago)"; Classic Chicago Magazine; Pullman State Historic Site / pullman-museum.org.

In 1861, Chicago lifted a six-story hotel into the air, with people still sleeping in it.
Chicago was built on a swamp, and the only way to fit sewers under the streets was to raise the whole city.
The biggest job was the Tremont House, a brick hotel downtown.
A young man named George Pullman promised he could lift it without disturbing a single guest or breaking a single window.
He slid thousands of jackscrews under the building, with hundreds of men standing beside them.
On a signal, every man gave his screws a half turn. Then another. And another.
Inch by inch, the whole hotel rose.
Luckily, the guests didn't have to check out.
Unluckily, the front steps now led up to a hotel that kept moving.
You see, one jack can lift a car with a few turns of your wrist.
Thousands of them, all turned together, can lift a building, as long as no corner goes up faster than the others.
Six feet later, the Tremont House sat on the new street, without a single broken window.
And what did the man who could lift a hotel without waking anyone do next?
He built the sleeping car: a hotel room on wheels.

## 3. The 125 Million Dollar Mix-Up
Facts: Mars Climate Orbiter, launched Dec 1998, lost at Mars arrival Sept 1999. Lockheed Martin (Denver) software
produced thruster impulse in pound-force seconds; JPL navigation software read the numbers as newton-seconds
(1 lbf = 4.45 N). The accumulated error put the orbiter far too low; it was lost. About $125 million.
Sources: LA Times (1999-10-01); NASA Mishap Investigation Board summary (via SimScale, The Weather Network).

In 1999, NASA lost a spacecraft at Mars because two teams used different units.
The Mars Climate Orbiter had flown for nine months to get there.
One team built its thrusters, in Colorado. Another team steered it, in California.
The first team measured every push in pounds. The second team read every number as newtons.
A pound of push is about four and a half newtons.
So every small nudge the spacecraft made was four and a half times bigger than the navigators thought.
Nudge after nudge, for months.
When it finally reached Mars, it swung in far too low, and it was never heard from again.
Luckily, nobody was on board.
Unluckily, it cost a hundred and twenty-five million dollars.
You see, a number without a unit is just a guess.
A recipe that says "add two" could mean two spoons, or two buckets.
After that, NASA made sure every team wrote down its units.
And the orbiter's job had been to study the atmosphere of Mars.
It got a very close look.

## 4. The War Australia Lost to Birds
Facts: Western Australia, Nov-Dec 1932. ~20,000 emus on wheat farms. Major G.P.W. Meredith, Royal Australian
Artillery, two Lewis guns, 10,000 rounds. By 8 Nov, 2,500 rounds for ~50 birds (estimates 50-200). A gun mounted on a
truck could not keep up over rough ground ⚠ (general knowledge). Meredith: emus "could face machine guns with the
invulnerability of tanks". Final claim: 986 emus for 9,860 rounds. The emu is on Australia's coat of arms ⚠ (general).
Sources: Wikipedia "Emu War"; History Hit; Australian Geographic; Scientific American blog.

In 1932, Australia sent soldiers with machine guns to fight birds. The birds won.
Twenty thousand emus had moved onto the wheat farms of Western Australia, and the farmers asked the army for help.
The army sent Major Meredith, a few soldiers, two machine guns and ten thousand bullets.
The emus split up into small groups and ran.
The soldiers put a machine gun on a truck. The emus outran the truck.
After the first week, two and a half thousand bullets had been fired, and about fifty birds were down.
Luckily, no soldiers were hurt.
Unluckily, their pride was.
You see, an emu can run around fifty kilometres an hour, and it is very hard to stop.
The major said they could face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks.
The army pulled out.
Meredith counted nine hundred and eighty-six emus for nine thousand eight hundred and sixty bullets.
That's ten bullets a bird.
And the emu is still on Australia's coat of arms.

## 5. The Day Pepsi Got Warships
Facts: Soviet roubles could not be spent abroad, so PepsiCo was paid in goods, chiefly Stolichnaya vodka (1970s deal).
1989: a deal for 17 old submarines and a cruiser, a frigate and a destroyer, resold for scrap (NYT, May 1989). PepsiCo's
head told President Bush's national security adviser: "We're disarming the Soviet Union faster than you are."
The "sixth-largest navy" claim is disputed, so it is not used.
Sources: History.com (Christopher Klein); Atlas Obscura; Amusing Planet; New York Times (1989-05-10) via secondary.

In 1989, Pepsi got paid in Soviet submarines.
Pepsi had been selling cola in the Soviet Union for years, but Soviet money couldn't be spent outside the country.
So Pepsi was paid in things instead. Mostly vodka.
Then the deal got bigger.
Seventeen old submarines, a cruiser, a frigate and a destroyer.
On paper, a soft drink company now had warships.
Luckily, they never had to use them.
Unluckily, nobody at Pepsi knew how to sail a submarine.
So they sold the whole fleet for scrap.
Pepsi's boss went to the White House and told the president's security adviser:
"We're disarming the Soviet Union faster than you are."
You see, if your money only works in one shop, you get paid in whatever that shop has.
Sometimes that's vodka.
Sometimes it's a destroyer.
So the Cold War arms race had a surprise winner: the cola war.

## 6. The Morning Sweden Switched Sides
Facts: Sweden drove on the left; neighbours drove on the right; most Swedish cars were left-hand drive ⚠ (general).
Sunday 3 Sept 1967 ("Dagen H", Högertrafik). Non-essential traffic banned 01:00-06:00; switch at 05:00 on a radio
countdown. 157 minor accidents that day; the next Monday had 125 accidents vs 130-198 on previous Mondays, none fatal.
Insurance claims fell about 40% afterwards; lower speed limits helped. Iceland switched in May 1968 ⚠ (general).
Sources: Wikipedia "Dagen H"; Mental Floss; Popular Science; 99% Invisible.

In 1967, a whole country switched which side of the road it drives on, in one morning.
Sweden drove on the left. Every country around it drove on the right.
And most Swedes were already driving cars with the steering wheel on the left.
So on Sunday, the third of September, all private traffic was banned from one in the morning.
At five o'clock, on a countdown over the radio, every car in Sweden carefully crossed to the other side of the road.
Everyone expected chaos.
Luckily, that Monday had fewer crashes than a normal Monday.
Unluckily for the doom-sayers, so did the months after that.
Insurance claims dropped by forty percent.
You see, when something feels dangerous, you slow down and pay attention.
For a while, everyone in Sweden drove like it was their first lesson.
The next year, Iceland did the same thing.

## 7. The Leaning Tower Is Also Bent
Facts: Pisa, construction began 1173. By the second floor (~1178) the ground on one side began to sink. Wars stopped
work for almost a century; the pause likely let the soil consolidate and saved the tower. Work resumed 1272 (Giovanni di
Simone); upper floors built taller on the sinking side to compensate, giving the tower a curve. Bell chamber finished 1372.
Sources: Wikipedia "Leaning Tower of Pisa"; New World Encyclopedia; Social Studies for Kids.

The Leaning Tower of Pisa isn't just leaning. It's bent.
Building started in 1173, on soft, wet ground.
Five years later, when they reached the second floor, one side began to sink.
Then a war stopped the work for almost a hundred years.
Luckily, that long pause let the soil underneath settle, which probably kept the tower from falling over.
Unluckily, when the builders came back, it was still leaning.
So they built the new floors taller on the low side, to point the top back up.
You see, it's like stacking books on a wonky table: you slip something under one side to make the next one level.
But the tower kept sinking.
So every fix became a new bend.
It took about two hundred years to finish.
So the most famous mistake in architecture is really several mistakes, each one trying to fix the one before.

## 8. The Toads That Ate Everything Except Beetles
Facts: Cane beetles damaged Queensland sugar cane roots. 102 cane toads (51 male, 51 female) came from Hawaii, arriving
at Gordonvale on 22 June 1935; one died in transit. Bred in captivity; ~2,400 released in Aug 1935. Ineffective: greyback
beetles rarely came near the ground, and French's beetles were active when the toads were not. Toads now spread west
40-60 km a year across northern Australia.
Sources: Wikipedia "Cane toads in Australia"; National Museum of Australia "Defining Moments"; EBSCO Research Starters.

In 1935, Australia brought in toads to eat beetles. The toads ate everything except the beetles.
Cane beetles were destroying Queensland's sugar cane, and someone had an idea: toads eat beetles.
So a hundred and two toads were shipped over from Hawaii.
They had babies, and that August, two thousand four hundred young toads were let loose in the fields.
Problem one: the beetles lived up on the cane stalks, and the toads lived on the ground.
Problem two: the beetles came out when the toads were asleep.
So the toads ate everything else instead.
Luckily, toads breed fast.
Unluckily, toads breed fast.
You see, a hunter only works if it actually meets what it's hunting.
It's like hiring a goalkeeper for a basketball team.
Today, there are millions of cane toads, spreading west by up to sixty kilometres a year.
All of them started with those hundred and two from Hawaii.

## 9. The Letter That Came by Missile
Facts: 8 June 1959. Submarine USS Barbero, about 100 miles off Florida, fired a Regulus I cruise missile carrying about
3,000 letters (no warhead) to the Naval Auxiliary Air Station at Mayport, Florida; flight about 22 minutes. Postmaster
General Arthur Summerfield called it "of historic significance to the peoples of the entire world". The only time the
US sent mail by missile (Smithsonian National Postal Museum).
Sources: Smithsonian National Postal Museum; USPS history "Missile Mail" (PDF); Mental Floss; Task & Purpose.

In 1959, the United States delivered the mail by cruise missile.
The Post Office wanted faster mail, and the Navy had a submarine with a missile on it.
So on June the eighth, off the coast of Florida, the submarine Barbero loaded a missile.
Instead of a warhead, it carried three thousand letters.
It launched, flew about a hundred miles over the sea, and twenty-two minutes later it landed at a naval air station in Mayport.
The Postmaster General called it historic, for the peoples of the entire world.
Luckily, every letter arrived.
Unluckily, every delivery needed a missile.
You see, mail is cheap because one truck carries thousands of letters for a tank of fuel.
A missile is a very fast, very expensive delivery van that only makes one trip.
So the future of mail lasted exactly one flight.
It's still the only time America's mail has gone by missile.

## 10. The Smell That Fixed London
Facts: London's sewage drained into the Thames. Hot summer of 1858. Curtains/blinds of the Houses of Parliament
were soaked in chloride of lime to mask the smell, without success. Disraeli introduced the bill on 15 July 1858; it was
law by 2 August (18 days). Joseph Bazalgette built the new sewer system, taking almost 20 years; much of it is still in
use ⚠ (general knowledge).
Sources: Museum of London / London Museum "Great Stink of 1858"; Historic UK; Mental Floss; History.co.uk.

In 1858, London fixed its sewers because Parliament couldn't stand the smell.
For years, London's toilets had emptied straight into the River Thames.
Then came a very hot summer, and the river began to cook.
The Houses of Parliament sat right on the riverbank.
So the politicians soaked their curtains in chloride of lime, to block out the smell.
It didn't work.
They had been arguing about sewers for years.
Now they passed a new law in eighteen days.
Luckily, the smell was finally under their noses.
Unluckily for everyone else, it had been under theirs the whole time.
You see, a problem everybody shares gets fixed slowly.
A problem the people in charge can smell gets fixed fast.
An engineer named Joseph Bazalgette spent almost twenty years building London a brand new sewer system.
And much of it is still flushing today.
